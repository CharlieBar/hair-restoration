FUE vs FUT — choosing the right technique for you.

Both procedures move your own follicles. The differences are in extraction method, scarring, recovery, and which hairstyles you can wear afterward — and the right answer depends on your specific donor area, hair loss pattern, and lifestyle.

KATONA / JOURNAL · ENTRY 002

EDGEBROOK · CHICAGO

If you've researched hair transplantation, you've encountered the same two acronyms over and over: FUE (Follicular Unit Extraction) and FUT (Follicular Unit Transplantation). The internet has decided that one is "modern" and the other is "outdated." That's not how a surgeon thinks about it.

FUE and FUT are not competing technologies. They are different tools that solve different problems — and a properly trained hair restoration surgeon offers both. The right technique for you depends on variables your reading cannot account for: your donor area density, the hair you wear, the volume of grafts you actually need, and the trade-offs you're willing to accept.

This is how Dr. Katona thinks through the FUE vs FUT decision with each patient.

What both techniques have in common

Both FUE and FUT transplant the same biological material — your own follicular units, which are naturally-occurring groupings of one to four hairs. The transplanted follicles are biologically resistant to DHT (the hormone responsible for genetic hair loss), which is why transplanted hair grows for the rest of your life regardless of what your other hair does.

Both procedures are performed under local anesthesia. Both take a single surgical day. Both produce results that are visible at three to six months and final at twelve. The differences are in the front end — how the follicles are obtained from the donor area — not the back end.

FUE — the flagship

In FUE, individual follicular units are extracted one at a time using a tiny rotating punch — typically 0.7mm to 1.0mm in diameter. Each follicle is removed manually, sorted under microscope, and then transplanted into recipient sites in the thinning area.

What this means for the patient:

  • No linear scar. The donor area heals as thousands of tiny dots, invisible at any normal hair length — including a buzz cut.
  • Faster recovery. Most patients are back to desk work in 2 — 3 days, with the donor area cosmetically normal in 5 — 7 days.
  • No stitches. The micro-punctures heal without sutures.
  • Hairstyle freedom. You can wear your hair as short as you want — there is nothing to hide.

The trade-offs:

  • Lower graft yield per session. Manual extraction is slower than the strip technique, so the maximum number of grafts in a single session is generally lower.
  • Higher cost per graft. The labor intensity of individual extraction makes FUE more expensive on a per-graft basis.
  • Donor area must be partially shaved. Most FUE procedures require shaving the donor region to expose the follicles for extraction.

FUT — the strip technique

In FUT, a narrow strip of donor scalp is removed from the back of the head — typically 1cm wide and however long your case requires. The strip is then dissected under microscope into individual follicular units by a team of trained technicians, while the donor area is closed with sutures using trichophytic closure — a technique that allows hair to grow through the scar, minimizing visibility.

What this means for the patient:

  • Higher graft yield per session. Strip dissection is fast, allowing larger graft counts in a single surgical day.
  • Lower cost per graft. The efficiency of strip processing typically makes FUT 15 — 25% less expensive per graft.
  • No need to shave the head. Only the strip area is shaved, which is then immediately covered when surrounding hair is laid back over it. This makes FUT the preferred choice for many female patients.
  • Highest donor utilization. Strip technique can extract from the safest, most permanent donor follicles — typically yielding the highest-quality grafts.

The trade-offs:

  • Linear donor scar. A fine line where the strip was removed. Modern trichophytic closure makes this extremely concealable, but it must be hidden by surrounding hair — meaning patients who want to shave their heads completely should choose FUE.
  • Sutures. Removed at day 10 — 14.
  • Slightly longer initial recovery. Typical desk-work return is 3 — 5 days; full donor healing 10 — 14.

The third option — FUE/FUT hybrid

For patients with extensive hair loss who need maximum graft yield in a single procedure, Dr. Katona offers a hybrid approach combining both techniques in one surgical day. The strip is taken from the safest donor zone, while FUE harvests from secondary donor regions to maximize total yield without compromising either area.

This is uncommon — most surgeons offer FUE or FUT, not both, and certainly not in combination. Hybrid is the technique that makes restoration possible for patients who have been told elsewhere that they don't have enough donor area.

Patients arrive expecting to have already made the decision online. After consultation, about a third change their mind. The internet decides based on which technique sounds more modern. A surgeon decides based on what your donor area, your hair, and your goals actually require. — Dr. Arthur Katona, MD

The honest answer to "which is better?"

Neither is better. Both are excellent in the right hands and the right case. The wrong question is "FUE or FUT?" The right question is: what does my specific case require, and which technique optimizes for what I value most?

If you wear your hair very short and prioritize the absence of any scar, FUE is the better answer. If you have advanced hair loss and need maximum density, FUT or hybrid is the better answer. If you're a woman who doesn't want her head shaved, FUT is almost always the better answer. If you're somewhere in between, the trade-offs are nuanced enough that they deserve a real surgical evaluation.
