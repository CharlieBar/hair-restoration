Hair loss stages — the Norwood and Ludwig scales, decoded.

A patient-friendly walk through the two clinical scales surgeons use to classify hair loss — what each stage actually looks like, why it matters for treatment planning, and what realistic outcomes you can expect at every level.

KATONA / JOURNAL · ENTRY 001
WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 11 MIN READ/ UPDATED 05 / 01 / 26/ CATEGORY · PATIENT EDUCATION/ 27 YEARS · 20,000+ PROCEDURES/ WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 11 MIN READ/ UPDATED 05 / 01 / 26/
EDGEBROOK · CHICAGO

If you've ever searched "how bad is my hair loss," you've already encountered the Norwood scale — the seven-stage chart of male pattern baldness that has been the surgical standard since 1975. Its female counterpart, the Ludwig scale, measures the more diffuse pattern that hair loss takes in women. Together, these two scales are the language hair surgeons use to describe what they see.

This guide walks through both — what each stage actually means, why correctly classifying yours is the first decision in any treatment plan, and what realistic outcomes you can expect when you arrive in our consultation room having already done the homework.

Why a scale at all?

Hair loss is not binary. It progresses — sometimes for decades — and the right intervention at Norwood II is almost never the right intervention at Norwood VI. Without a shared vocabulary, surgeons would describe the same patient in five different ways. The Norwood and Ludwig scales solve that problem by giving every clinician — and every patient — the same anchor points.

For a patient, knowing your stage does three things:

  • It tells you how aggressively to consider intervention now
  • It predicts where your hair loss is most likely to go next
  • It establishes whether non-surgical treatment alone can produce the result you want, or whether surgery will eventually enter the conversation

The Norwood scale — for men

Developed by Dr. James Hamilton in 1951 and refined by Dr. O'Tar Norwood in 1975, the Norwood scale tracks androgenetic alopecia in seven stages, with a separate "Type A" variant for patients whose hairline recedes uniformly rather than developing the classic frontal "M" pattern.

Stage I — No significant loss

The reference baseline. A youthful, low frontal hairline with a full crown. Most men sit at Stage I until their late teens or early twenties. No intervention indicated.

Stage II — Slight temporal recession

The first signs of a maturing hairline — small triangular recessions at the temples. Many men at Stage II remain stable for life. Others continue to progress. This is the optimal stage to begin non-surgical maintenance if family history suggests progression — PRP, low-level light therapy, and Finasteride all have their strongest evidence in early-stage loss.

Stage III — Defined hairline recession

Recession deepens at the temples to form a more pronounced "M" shape. The frontal hairline is clearly retreating. Stage III is the inflection point at which most men first start to notice their hair loss — and the point at which a meaningful conversation about long-term strategy becomes appropriate.

Stage III Vertex — Recession plus crown thinning

Stage III with the addition of visible thinning at the crown (vertex). This pattern often progresses faster than non-vertex Stage III. Combination protocols — surgical or non-surgical — produce the strongest outcomes when started here.

Stage IV — Bridge of hair separates frontal and crown loss

The frontal recession and crown thinning have both progressed, but a "bridge" of hair still connects them across the top. Stage IV is typically where surgical hair restoration becomes a strong primary option — there is enough donor hair available and enough recipient area to design a meaningful restoration.

Stage V — Bridge thinning, the gap closing

The bridge between frontal and crown loss is now visibly thinning. Stage V is the upper bound of what most surgeons consider straightforward to restore in a single session — beyond this, multi-session or hybrid FUE/FUT approaches are usually required.

Stage VI — Bridge gone, frontal and crown loss merge

The hair on top of the head has largely receded. The donor area on the back and sides remains intact in most patients. Stage VI is restorable but typically requires either a hybrid technique or multiple sessions. This is the stage at which Dr. Katona's experience with hybrid FUE/FUT becomes most valuable — many patients told elsewhere that they have insufficient donor density find a different answer here.

Stage VII — Most advanced

Hair loss extends from the frontal region all the way back, with only a band of hair remaining around the sides and back. Realistic restoration at Stage VII requires careful management of expectations — full restoration to a Stage I appearance is rarely possible, but significant cosmetic improvement using maximum-density hairline reconstruction is achievable in well-selected patients.

The Norwood scale predicts what's likely to happen, but it doesn't predict when. A 25-year-old at Stage III who progresses to Stage VI by 35 has a fundamentally different treatment timeline than a 50-year-old at the same Stage III who has been stable for fifteen years. Family history, age of onset, and rate of progression all factor in. — Dr. Arthur Katona, MD

The Ludwig scale — for women

Female pattern hair loss takes a fundamentally different shape than male. Where men typically lose hair in patterns dictated by the Norwood scale (frontal recession, vertex thinning), women experience more diffuse thinning across the central scalp — usually preserving the frontal hairline.

The Ludwig scale describes this pattern in three stages:

Ludwig I — Mild diffuse thinning

Visible widening of the part line, particularly at the crown. The frontal hairline is preserved. Many women at Ludwig I notice the change before others do — typically when seeing photographs or looking down at their part. Non-surgical intervention is highly effective at this stage and should be initiated promptly.

Ludwig II — Moderate diffuse thinning

The widening progresses; scalp may be visible through the hair when wet or under direct light. The frontal hairline still typically remains. Combination protocols (PRP, LLLT, topical Minoxidil, hormonal evaluation) usually produce meaningful improvement; some women begin to consider surgical augmentation at this stage.

Ludwig III — Advanced diffuse thinning

Significant transparent thinning across the central scalp; the frontal hairline may begin to thin. Surgical restoration becomes a primary consideration, often using FUT (strip) technique to avoid the need to shave the head — most women at Ludwig III prefer this approach for cosmetic continuity.

Why your stage isn't the whole story

The Norwood and Ludwig scales describe the pattern of your hair loss. They do not describe its cause. Two patients can present at identical Norwood IV but require completely different treatment plans because the underlying mechanism is different.

At consultation, Dr. Katona evaluates four additional variables that the scales do not capture:

  • Donor area density — the number of follicles per cm² in the back and sides of your scalp. This is the single most important determinant of how much restoration is surgically possible.
  • Rate of progression — how fast you've moved between stages. Aggressive progression indicates a more aggressive treatment posture.
  • Age and family history — predicts where your hair loss is going, not just where it is now.
  • Underlying medical or hormonal contributors — thyroid disease, iron deficiency, post-pregnancy shedding, medication effects. Surgical correction without addressing these can produce poor results.

Where to go from here

If you've matched yourself to a Norwood or Ludwig stage on this page, the next step is a complete diagnostic evaluation — clinical assessment, donor area trichoscopy, and, when indicated, lab work to rule out medical contributors.
