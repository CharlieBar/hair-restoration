Why a dermatology-trained medical director matters at a medspa.

Most medical spas operate under physicians whose primary specialty is internal medicine, family practice, or emergency medicine. What three dermatology fellowships actually catch that other oversight misses.

KATONA / JOURNAL · ENTRY 003
WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 7 MIN READ/ CATEGORY · AESTHETIC MEDICINE/ 3 DERMATOLOGY FELLOWSHIPS/ 27 YEARS MEDICAL PRACTICE/ WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 7 MIN READ/ CATEGORY · AESTHETIC MEDICINE/
EDGEBROOK · CHICAGO

If you've booked Botox or filler at a medical spa, you may have noticed a small line on the back of the consent form: "Procedure performed under the medical direction of [Physician Name, MD]." What you almost certainly were not told is that physician's specialty.

State law requires medical spas offering medical-grade procedures to operate under physician oversight. The depth and quality of that oversight, however, varies dramatically. Some medical directors are minimally involved — a name on a consent form and a quarterly meeting. Others — like Dr. Katona at Essence — are actively engaged in protocol design, complex case review, and direct patient care.

The single biggest variable in quality of medical directorship is what the physician's primary specialty actually is.

The medspa medical director landscape

An informal survey of medspa medical directors in the Chicago area illustrates the issue. The majority hold board certifications in:

  • Internal Medicine
  • Family Practice
  • Emergency Medicine
  • Anesthesiology
  • OB/GYN

All of these are legitimate medical specialties. None of them have anything to do with skin biology, pigment conditions, allergic reactions, or suspicious lesion identification — which is, not coincidentally, what every medspa procedure operates on or just under.

This is the structural mismatch. The procedures performed at a medspa happen on or below the skin surface. The medical director who reviews the protocols, trains the providers, and consults on complications often has minimal training in skin medicine.

What this catches in practice

The value of dermatology training as medical directorship isn't theoretical. Here are the specific situations in which it matters most.

Pre-treatment evaluation

Before a Botox, filler, laser, or peel appointment, a routine medical evaluation typically clears the patient for treatment. A dermatology-trained physician's pre-treatment assessment looks at the same patient differently — examining the skin for unusual lesions, evaluating contraindications based on skin type and condition, and identifying underlying issues that might affect treatment outcomes.

The most common scenario: a patient books filler. During consultation, the dermatologist notices an asymmetric mole the patient has had for years and never thought twice about. It's biopsied the same week. Sometimes it's nothing. Sometimes it's an early melanoma that would have gone undiagnosed for another year or two.

Unexpected reactions

Aesthetic procedures occasionally produce reactions — bruising, swelling, allergic responses, vascular events. A general medical director addresses these with general medical knowledge. A dermatology-trained physician distinguishes between an expected post-procedure response, a true allergic reaction, an underlying skin condition that has been activated by the procedure, and a vascular complication that needs immediate intervention.

The clinical decision tree at the moment of an unexpected reaction is the difference between a problem that resolves in days and one that produces lasting damage.

Pigment conditions

Melasma is the canonical example. Aggressive treatment can dramatically worsen melasma — and many medspa protocols are aggressive by default. A dermatology-trained physician diagnoses the specific pigment condition, calibrates treatment intensity to avoid worsening, and builds combination protocols around the root cause rather than against the surface symptom.

This is why patients chasing melasma at multiple medspas often see no progress until they finally land with a dermatology-trained physician — at which point the protocol is reduced in aggression, sequenced more carefully, and finally produces results.

Suspicious lesions during routine treatment

The dermatology eye is trained to identify atypical lesions during what would otherwise be a cosmetic procedure. The standard medspa workflow does not include this check. The patient comes in for laser hair removal; the laser tech treats the area; the slightly atypical mole nearby is never noticed.

Skin cancer caught early is often a five-minute biopsy. Skin cancer caught late is often a much larger problem.

The difference between a medspa with a non-dermatology medical director and one with a dermatology-trained medical director is rarely visible on the marketing. It becomes visible in the cases that don't fit the standard protocol — and those are the cases where the difference matters most. — Dr. Arthur Katona, MD

The two-tier model at Essence

Dr. Katona's role as Medical Director at Essence operates on a two-tier model that we keep deliberately transparent — patients should always know who is performing their treatment.

Tier 1 — Performed personally by Dr. Katona: PRP/PRF for face, medical-grade chemical peels (TCA, Jessner's), skin cancer screening, sclerotherapy, and the medical management of acne, rosacea, and melasma. These are the services where his specific training applies most directly.

Tier 2 — Under Dr. Katona's medical direction: Botox and other neuromodulators, dermal fillers, bio-stimulators, PDO threads, microneedling, lasers, and Kybella. These services are performed by Essence's licensed aesthetic providers — registered nurses, physician assistants, and certified injectors. Dr. Katona's role is to review and approve every protocol they use, train and credential them, evaluate the products and devices being deployed, and be available for direct consultation on complex cases.

This model is how every reputable medspa operates. The difference at Essence is the depth of the medical training overseeing it.

The practical takeaway

If you're booking aesthetic treatment at a medical spa, ask one question: "What is the medical director's specialty?"

If the answer is internal medicine, family practice, emergency medicine, or anesthesiology — those are legitimate specialties, but they are not skin medicine. The protocols they oversee are still legal and (when properly executed) safe, but the depth of expertise behind that oversight is more limited than the marketing usually implies.

If the answer is dermatology — at any level — the oversight is more substantively connected to what is actually happening to your skin. If the answer is three dermatology fellowships plus 27 years of clinical practice, you are at Essence.
