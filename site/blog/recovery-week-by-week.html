Hair transplant recovery — what to expect, week by week.

From procedure day through the twelfth month — what's normal, what's not, and the milestones every patient should anticipate. Realistic expectations are the foundation of a satisfied result.

KATONA / JOURNAL · ENTRY 004
WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 8 MIN READ/ CATEGORY · PATIENT GUIDE/ RECOVERY · DAY 0 → MONTH 12/ WHAT TO EXPECT/ WRITTEN BY DR. KATONA/ 8 MIN READ/ CATEGORY · PATIENT GUIDE/
EDGEBROOK · CHICAGO

The single most common reason patients become unhappy with their hair transplant is not the result — it is the distance between what they expected and what actually happened in the months in between. Hair grows slowly. Transplanted hair sheds before it grows back. The first three months feel like nothing is happening. None of this is a complication. All of it is normal.

This guide walks you through the first year of recovery, day-by-day for the first week, then week-by-week and month-by-month after that. Save it. Refer back to it. The patients who do best post-procedure are the ones who know what's coming before it arrives.

Day 0 — procedure day

You arrive in the morning. Local anesthesia is administered using a small injection cycle — most patients describe minimal discomfort, often easier than a dental appointment. Dr. Katona performs the donor extraction, recipient site creation, and graft placement personally. You're awake throughout. You can read, watch movies, or rest. Lunch is provided.

By end of day, you'll have tiny crusts on the recipient sites (where the grafts were placed) and either dot-pattern micro-scabs (FUE) or a fine sutured incision (FUT) at the donor area. You'll be sent home with detailed aftercare instructions and a sleep wedge to keep your head elevated for the first few nights.

What to expect that night:

  • Mild tightness at the donor area
  • Possible mild swelling (uncommon, but normal if it appears)
  • The recipient sites look like tiny red dots — this is the graft itself
  • Pain is typically minimal — over-the-counter Tylenol is sufficient for most

Days 1 — 3 — early healing

You'll see Dr. Katona for a check-in the morning after surgery (or virtual follow-up for out-of-town patients). He'll inspect the grafts, confirm healing is on track, and answer any questions.

  • Sleep with your head elevated for the first 3 — 4 nights to minimize swelling
  • No bending forward, no exercise, no alcohol for the first 72 hours
  • Mild swelling may appear on the forehead or around the eyes — peaks at day 3, resolves by day 5 — 7
  • Most patients return to desk work at day 3; some take a full week off for cosmetic reasons

Days 5 — 7 — first wash

Around day 5 — 7 you'll do your first proper hair wash following our specific protocol — gentle, no scrubbing of the recipient area. The crusts on the grafts begin to soften and detach over the following days. Do not pick at them. They will come off naturally.

By the end of week one, the donor area looks essentially normal in FUE. FUT donor area still shows the suture line, scheduled for removal at day 10 — 14.

Weeks 2 — 3 — the shed

Here is the milestone that surprises patients more than any other: around week 2 — 3, the transplanted hairs begin to fall out. All of them. By the end of week 3, your transplanted area can look very much like it did before the procedure.

This is normal. This is expected. This is not a failed transplant.

What's actually happening: the visible hair shaft sheds, but the follicle — the part that was transplanted — remains permanently in place beneath the scalp. It enters a dormant phase before producing the first new hair growth.

The shed at weeks 2 — 3 is the most psychologically difficult moment of the entire recovery. Every patient gets through it. The patients who do best are the ones who knew it was coming. — Dr. Arthur Katona, MD

Months 1 — 2 — the waiting

Not much visible happens in months 1 and 2. The follicles are present beneath the scalp, going through their dormant phase. The donor area is fully healed by week 4 — 6 (FUE) or week 6 — 8 (FUT). Trichophytic closure makes the FUT linear scar very fine — it becomes progressively less visible over the following 12 — 18 months.

You can resume full exercise around week 3 — 4, including weights and cardio. Hats and helmets are fine after week 2. Sun exposure to the recipient area should be avoided for the first month — wear a hat or use sunscreen.

Month 3 — first growth

Around month 3, the first new hair growth appears. Initially it's fine, thin, and lighter in color than your final hair will be. Don't judge the result yet — these early hairs are placeholders. Texture and thickness develop over the next 6 — 9 months.

This is also when patients tend to start feeling encouraged again. Visible new hair, even if fine, signals that the procedure is working as expected.

Months 4 — 6 — density develops

Through months 4, 5, and 6, the new hair thickens, darkens, and gains length. Significant cosmetic density appears at month 6 — most patients are comfortable being seen in any social setting at this point. Photographs at month 6 still don't represent the final result, but the trajectory is unmistakable.

Months 7 — 9 — continued maturation

The transplanted hair continues to thicken and texture evens out. Months 7 — 9 are typically when patients stop thinking about their transplant on a daily basis — the hair simply becomes part of how they look.

Month 12 — the final result

By the end of month 12, the transplanted hair has reached its final density, thickness, and texture. This is the appropriate time to evaluate the result. Photographs taken before the procedure should be compared to month 12 photographs — that comparison is the honest measure of the procedure's success.

From here on, the transplanted hair grows naturally and permanently for the rest of your life. It is biologically resistant to DHT — the hormone responsible for genetic hair loss — which is why it does not thin or fall out the way the surrounding native hair might.

What about the native hair?

Hair transplant surgery moves the hair you have. It does not stop hair loss in the hair that wasn't transplanted. This is why long-term post-transplant maintenance — typically PRP, low-level light therapy, or medical therapy with Finasteride — is recommended for nearly all transplant patients.

Without ongoing maintenance, your native hair can continue to thin around healthy transplanted follicles, eventually producing the appearance of dense islands within thinning surroundings. With maintenance, both the transplanted and native hair continue to look natural together for decades.

This is a conversation we have at consultation, before procedure day — well before the question of "what next" becomes urgent.

The honest summary

Hair transplantation is a procedure with a long timeline. The first three months feel slow. The shed at weeks 2 — 3 is psychologically hard. The first new growth at month 3 is encouraging but not the final result. The real evaluation happens at month 12.

Patients who know all of this in advance navigate the recovery comfortably. Patients who learn it as they go often experience an unnecessary period of doubt around weeks 3 — 8.

If you're considering hair transplantation, bookmark this guide. Refer back to it. Most of the questions you'll have post-procedure are already answered above.
